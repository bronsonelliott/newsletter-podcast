Good morning, and welcome to Morning Token. It's a short one today, but there's plenty to chew on before your first coffee gets cold.

Let's start with the money, because it's always the money. Halcyon Labs announced a $2.1 billion funding round led by Brackwater Capital. Halcyon makes AI for hospital scheduling, which is about as unglamorous as AI gets, and that's exactly why I find it interesting. Nobody writes breathless headlines about who gets the 9:15 MRI slot, but every hospital administrator knows how much time and money goes into juggling those calendars by hand. Software that takes that work off their plates has a clear buyer and a clear reason to exist.

Still, a round that size makes me want to ask what the company plans to do with it. Scheduling is a problem where the return on investment is easy to explain, and that's a rare thing in this market. The real test will be whether Halcyon can keep that focus once the cash arrives and everyone in the room starts pushing for a bigger story. Boring businesses can get very exciting for investors when the checks get large, and the best founders resist that pull.

Now to open models. Northwind released Kestrel-12B, an open model with 12 billion parameters, under the Apache 2.0 license. That license lets companies use it commercially, modify it, and ship it inside their own products without asking permission or paying a royalty. For teams that have been stuck choosing between a closed API they don't control and a model with murky terms, this is a real option, and it's the kind of release that quietly changes what small companies can afford to build.

I read this as a signal about where the middle of the market is heading. Not every company needs the largest model available. A 12 billion parameter model that a small team can run and tune on its own hardware may be the more sensible choice for a lot of everyday work, like classification, summarization, and internal search. Whether Kestrel-12B holds up against the competition in practice is a question for your own testing, not the press release. Run it on your real data before you make any decisions.

Verity has cut the price of its API by 40 percent, effective Monday. If you build on Verity, that's good news for your next invoice, and you should probably spend a few minutes checking whether your usage patterns now make a different plan worthwhile. If you build on someone else, it's a reminder that API prices are now something providers adjust deliberately to win customers, and your renewal conversations should reflect that. Negotiating used to feel like a once-a-year chore. It's becoming a standing part of the job.

Price cuts this large tend to get read as a sign of pressure, and it's worth thinking about what that means for the whole category. Cheaper inference makes more products economical to build, which is good for developers and good for users who have been waiting for features to make sense financially. It also squeezes anyone whose margins depended on the old prices staying put. My guess is we'll see more of these moves before the year is out, and some companies will decide that competing on price is easier than competing on everything else.

Finally, Fennel said its coding agent has passed 1 million paying users. That's a striking number for a product in a crowded field, and it says developers are willing to pay for tools that do real work inside their repositories rather than just autocomplete a line or two. A year ago, many teams treated these tools as toys. Now they're being asked to justify a monthly line item, and a million people have decided the answer is yes.

I'm less interested in the headline figure than in what those users are asking the agent to do. If the most common tasks are the tedious ones, like migrations, test writing, and dependency updates, then these agents are finding their place in the workflow, and that's a healthy sign. If the tasks are more ambitious, the bar for everyone else goes up. Either way, the question for engineering managers is no longer whether to allow these tools, but how to measure whether they're actually saving time.
