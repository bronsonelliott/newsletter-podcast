Counsel & Code

Welcome back to Counsel & Code. It has been a busy stretch for anyone who spends their days at the intersection of machine learning and the law, and this issue opens with three developments that, taken together, sketch out where the field is heading.

The first is a ruling in Meridian v. the Writers Cooperative, where a judge held that training on copyrighted books was fair use. The decision is a win for Meridian AI, which has been defending its training practices against a case brought by the Writers Cooperative. For a lot of us, the question of whether ingesting copyrighted text to build a model counts as fair use has been the central open question of the past few years, and a judge putting a clear answer on paper changes the conversation. Lawyers on both sides will now be reading the opinion closely, looking for the reasoning that will carry into their own matters. I'd caution against treating one ruling as settled law, but it is hard to overstate how much this shifts the negotiating posture for everyone who licenses, sues, or advises on training data. Expect a wave of settlement talks and a wave of briefs that cite this case in their first few pages.

The second item is the Ostrava Accord, a voluntary safety pledge that fourteen companies have signed. The commitments center on testing large models before they are released. It is a voluntary arrangement, which means it has no enforcement mechanism of the kind a statute would bring, and I think it is fair to be skeptical of what voluntary frameworks can accomplish on their own. Still, I find the document more interesting as a signal than as a rulebook. When fourteen companies agree in writing to a common testing standard, they create a reference point. Regulators can point to it, plaintiffs can measure conduct against it, and in-house counsel can use it to justify budget requests for evaluation teams that previously struggled to get funding. Voluntary commitments often become the raw material for mandatory rules later, so those of us advising clients should read the Accord's text carefully and ask which of its language a court or agency might eventually borrow.

The third item is about the business of legal tech itself. Quill, which makes AI tools for court reporters, raised $85 million at a $600 million valuation. That is a meaningful bet on the idea that transcription and record-making in courtrooms is a market worth serious capital, and it tells us that investors see the courthouse as more than a slow-moving customer. Court reporting is a profession with a precise and demanding output: a verbatim record that must hold up on appeal. Any tool that touches that record has to earn the trust of judges, attorneys, and the reporters themselves, who have good reason to worry that automation will hollow out their jobs. I'd watch how Quill handles that tension. The companies that succeed in this space will probably be the ones that make reporters more capable rather than the ones that present themselves as replacements.

Taken together, these three stories point in a consistent direction. Courts are starting to answer questions about how AI systems may be built. Industry is writing its own rules about how they should be tested. And capital is flowing into the tools that will sit inside the legal system's everyday machinery. Each of these developments creates work for lawyers, whether that means advising on fair use exposure, reviewing voluntary commitments for contractual implications, or vetting new vendors for the courtroom. The gap between what the technology can do and what the profession has agreed to accept is where most of our readers earn their keep.

The first problem most licensing drafts run into is that they treat the license as a permission slip when it is really a map of risk. A licensor who grants rights to a dataset for model training is usually trying to do two things at once: get paid, and avoid being the party whose name appears in a complaint. Those goals pull in different directions, and the clauses that serve one often undercut the other.

Start with the definition of the licensed material. Too many agreements describe the dataset as "text, images, and related content," which sounds generous but gives a court almost nothing to work with. If a dispute arises, both sides will argue about what was actually delivered, when, and in what form. A better draft lists the source collections by name, states the date ranges covered, and includes a schedule of exclusions for material the licensor knows it does not have rights to. That exclusions schedule is the single most underused tool in these deals. It shows good faith, and it narrows the field of material where a fair use argument will be needed in the first place.

Next comes the purpose clause, which is where drafters most often get lazy. A license that says the data may be used "for training and improving machine learning models" is broad enough to cover almost anything, and broad purpose language is exactly what a licensor's counsel should resist. Think about what the licensee is likely to do in year three. Will they fine-tune a model for a customer? Build a retrieval product on top of it? Release model weights? Each of those activities has a different risk profile, and each affects how a fair use analysis might go, because courts look closely at the purpose and character of the use. The license should name the permitted activities and reserve the rest for renegotiation.

Then there is the question of outputs, which many agreements handle as an afterthought. If the licensee's model reproduces a passage from the training material, who bears the consequences? A workable approach is to require the licensee to maintain output filtering appropriate to the risk, to notify the licensor within a fixed period of any claim involving reproduced material, and to give the licensor a defined role in responding. Notice that none of this requires the parties to agree on whether the use was fair. It only requires them to agree on who does what when someone asks.

Indemnity deserves its own paragraph, because it is where the money lives. A licensor who indemnifies the licensee for all third-party claims is taking on a risk it cannot price. A licensor who disclaims everything is selling a dataset that may be worth less than the paper it is printed on. The compromise that tends to hold up is a capped indemnity limited to claims arising from the licensor's own representations about provenance, paired with a warranty that the licensor has disclosed any known third-party restrictions. The cap should be tied to the fees paid, not to an arbitrary number, and the drafters should think hard about carve-outs for willful misconduct, because courts often will not enforce caps that shield deliberate wrongdoing.

Audit rights are the part nobody wants to negotiate and everybody later wishes they had. A licensor should be able to verify, through a neutral third party if necessary, that the data is stored and used as the license allows. Build the audit clause so it is proportionate. A demand to inspect every training run is a sign the licensor doesn't trust the relationship, and it will make the licensee dig in. A clause that lets an independent reviewer confirm the dataset's location, access list, and retention schedule once a year is a much easier thing to sign.

Finally, plan for the end of the relationship. Termination clauses in data deals typically say what happens to the data, but they rarely say what happens to models trained on it. Licensors and licensees need to decide in advance whether a trained model must be retired, retrained, or may survive termination, and on what terms. Leaving that question open is the drafting equivalent of leaving the keys in the car. The parties will not agree on it when the relationship is souring, so it has to be settled while everyone is still friendly.

None of this guarantees that a license will survive a fair use challenge, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. What good drafting does is make the dispute narrower, the responsibilities clearer, and the lawyers' phone calls shorter. That is most of what the job is.

The warranty section is where good intentions go to die. Licensors want a promise that the data was lawfully collected, and licensees want to promise as little as possible. The usual compromise is a representation that the licensor has "all necessary rights," which sounds reassuring until you ask which rights, under which jurisdiction, and as of when. A provenance warranty that is specific about sources is worth ten generic ones. If the dataset includes scraped material, the contract should say so, list the collection methods, and state whether robots.txt directives and platform terms were honored at the time.

Indemnities tend to get the same treatment. Licensees ask for the licensor to cover third-party claims arising from the data, and licensors counter with a cap equal to the fees paid in the prior twelve months. Those two positions can both be reasonable and still produce a deal that protects nobody, because the claims that matter most, such as a publisher suing over copying at scale, can dwarf any annual fee. I have seen parties agree on a generous indemnity and then quietly carve it out for claims tied to model outputs. Read the carve-outs first. They are where the real allocation of risk lives.

My practical advice is to write the warranty around facts you can check and to pair it with a notice obligation. If a claim arrives, the party that learns of it first should have a duty to tell the other within a fixed number of days. Silence is the most expensive thing in these relationships, and a short notice clause costs almost nothing to draft.
