Good morning, it's Tuesday, November twenty-fourth, and Jensen Huang is back at work at Nvidia.

On Sunday, Nvidia's board voted unanimously to reinstate Huang as president, chief executive and director, and it announced the decision Monday. He had resigned on November twelfth over affairs, including one with a direct report, according to The Rundown, and one with a woman US officials suspect of working for Chinese intelligence. Beijing has denied the spying claim. On November seventeenth, the board said it did not intend to bring him back and cited a lack of candor. Now it says it's satisfied with his candor and that his return poses no national security risk. The Neuron points out that the board hasn't explained what changed in between.

President Trump is happy to explain. He told CNBC, "I made the deal," and said he worked on it with Vice President Vance, Treasury Secretary Bessent and Commerce Secretary Lutnick. TLDR reports that Trump personally promised Huang hadn't broken national security laws. CNN describes a weekend pressure campaign, with Vance calling directors. Semafor and CNN both say Trump dialed into a board meeting. Asked about that on air, he said only that he was "represented." Bloomberg's headline says the board acted without consulting management.

The market reaction was enormous. Nvidia closed up about thirty-one and a half percent, at a hundred sixty-five dollars and five cents. AI Secret and AI Valley put the one-day gain at roughly nine hundred fifty billion dollars, which AI Valley says is about double the previous record for any company. The Rundown says it passed a trillion during trading. The newsletters disagree on whether that undoes the damage. AI Daily Brief says Nvidia has erased its losses since Huang left, while AI Valley says it got back most of them. Either way, it's still far below last month's high of two hundred thirty-nine dollars.

Some things stay changed. Harvey Jones remains board chair, and Colette Kress goes back to being chief financial officer. Nvidia is creating a committee on national security and export control, and hiring a chief compliance officer. The independent investigation continues, so this is a job offer rather than an exoneration. Latent Space passes along Matt Levine's point that the board has now fired and rehired Huang before its investigators finished, which makes their job awkward.

Washington is the next stop. The Neuron has Huang sitting for a transcribed interview with three House committees on December first, Nvidia's general counsel following on the second, and Huang appearing before Senate Banking on the third. Speaker-designate Hakeem Jeffries called the reinstatement "indefensible, irresponsible, and incomprehensible." Republican national security chairs say the board's brief probe doesn't erase the threat. A YouGov poll found fifty-four percent of Americans think the affair definitely or probably compromised national security. And several newsletters say the Commerce Department has put Megaspeed, the Singapore data center company where the woman used to work, on its trade blacklist over suspected smuggling of Nvidia chips to China. Techpresso describes that as still planned.

The opinions split predictably. The Neuron thinks Nvidia is stronger with Huang, though it says the board owes shareholders a clear account. In The Deep View, Infotech analyst Brian Jackson says the reversal looks like a cave-in to outside pressure, and that Nvidia's valuation seems to rest on a cult of personality around one man in a leather jacket. For the record, AI Valley reports he skipped the leather jacket on his first day. He wore a navy work jacket, handed out mini sandwiches, and promised to be a better CEO the second time around. AI Valley's author had written that Huang wouldn't return before the investigations ended, and opened with "I was wrong." That's more candor than most of this story has offered.

Next, Anthropic launched Claude Ship on Monday. It's a set of agents that take over after the code has been written. There are three parts. Code Review flags bugs and is already generally available. PR Steward fixes failing checks and merge conflicts and responds to reviewer comments. Claude Approvals signs off on routine changes under a policy your team writes in plain English and keeps in the repository. Those last two are in beta for Team and Enterprise plans on GitHub. You type slash ship in Claude Code, and it opens the pull request, reviews it, and watches it for up to seven days.

There's one detail the newsletters don't agree on. The AI Report says Claude Approvals can merge routine changes on its own. Latent Space and AlphaSignal both say your branch rules still decide what merges. Model Radar adds that PR Steward is designed not to approve or merge anything, and that Ship isn't available to organizations using zero data retention, HIPAA compliance, or their own encryption keys. Anthropic's own advice is cautious. Start in observe-only mode, use one repository, and set a monthly spending limit.

Anthropic's reasoning is that agents now write code faster than people can review it. By coincidence or otherwise, Anthropic's economists published a study the same day that fits. Intelligence by Intent summarizes it. They ran sixty-six interviews at fifty-eight US companies that use Anthropic's products. Checking AI output came up as an obstacle a hundred and one times. Regulation came up four times.

The same study found that only two of the fifty-eight companies saw AI eliminate a US job, but two-thirds hired fewer people because of it. One leader said, "We've added a lot of capacity by buying tokens instead of hiring people." The newsletter's author, Stephen Smith, worries about what that does to training. His example is law. If firms skip junior hires now, he argues, by twenty thirty-one they'll be short of senior lawyers who can catch an AI's mistakes. He also says to take the sample with salt, since these are Anthropic customers and Anthropic sells the tools.

Third, OpenAI published a number it used to guard. Chief scientist Jakub Pachocki released an essay called "Perplexity and Intelligence," with five years of scores from OpenAI's base models. The measure is log-perplexity, roughly how surprised a model is by the next chunk of text it hasn't seen. Lower is better. On OpenAI's internal coding dataset, GPT three point five was around point nine nine. GPT four was around point nine one. GPT six Astra is around point seven six.

OpenAI says dropping below point eight coincided with new abilities in cybersecurity and mathematics. It expects another jump somewhere around point six eight to point seven. An unreleased internal model is already below the released Astra. One unofficial outside estimate has OpenAI reaching point seven between January and March.

Pachocki's proposal is that labs agree on a common limit and spell out what would let them cross it, such as a better way of monitoring what models are doing. Sam Altman called it the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while. Microsoft joined in first. Mustafa Suleyman's team ran its MAI two base model on OpenAI's test, with permission, and reported point eight one nine. Latent Space says it would take a couple more top labs, especially Anthropic, before this looks like real self-regulation. Anthropic redacted its pretraining metrics from its report for the evaluation group METR, and researchers are now asking for them.

The critics have fair questions. Some say differences in how models chop up text could favor OpenAI over Anthropic. Others say post-training probably accounts for more than three quarters of the computing, so a pretraining number may be the wrong thing to cap. Another suggestion is that model-generated training data explains part of the steep curve. OpenAI staff answer that the test is held out, frozen, and excludes model-generated code. Several researchers would like to see the test for themselves.

This comes as Axios, summarized by Techpresso, reports that staff at OpenAI and Anthropic believe their internal models may be too powerful to release safely. That's based on more than two dozen sources. Meta's Alexandr Wang also posted an essay declaring that superintelligence is arriving, and urging the industry off its default path. The Rundown calls that quite a departure from his usual tone.

Fourth, mathematicians are still digesting the claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the conjecture about prime numbers that dates to eighteen fifty-nine. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge says he found the proof working with Claude. AI Secret says he posted it the day after Anthropic's IPO. There's no paper, just seventeen million lines in Lean, the software mathematicians use to check proofs step by step. Lean accepted it on November twenty-first.

The Lean and Mathlib communities say they have full confidence. Independent checkers reportedly verified it too. AI Daily Brief adds the caution: a Lean proof is as fallible as any other program, some people claim to have found bugs, and it shouldn't be treated as rock solid until there's more verification.

The deeper argument is about understanding. AI Secret reports that nobody, including Alpöge, can yet explain why the proof works. Kevin Buzzard of Imperial College London said, "At the minute, we don't even know why the theorem is true." Ngo Bao Chau argues it isn't truly proved until humans understand it. The Association for Human Mathematics was harsher, calling it a corporate model of proof production designed to excuse humans from responsibility.

A second claim is circulating as well. A proof credited to Claude, in Anthropic's formal math collection, says that two classes called VP and VNP differ over every field. That's the algebraic cousin of the P versus NP problem. World of AI notes that no human mathematician has checked it, and that it doesn't settle P versus NP itself.

Fifth, the Chinese lab Z dot A I released GLM five point four, its first flagship built as a looped transformer. It has seven hundred fifty billion parameters, and it runs the same twenty-eight layers twice. The idea is to reason more deeply without making the model bigger. Z dot A I claims it performs like models of two to three trillion parameters. Its demos include a music video, a playable game, and 3D models, all produced by writing code.

What it didn't release is benchmark scores, and the skeptics noticed. "No benches equals bad benches" was one response. It's available on the company's coding plan, from eighteen dollars a month. The API and open weights come later, after safety work. World of AI says early users on Reddit report strong coding, weak general writing, and occasional bouts of marathon thinking.

It follows Inkling two Mini from Thinking Machines, a much smaller looped model with open weights. Reviews there are mixed. One Reddit tester scored it below a similar-sized Qwen model on nine private coding bugs, though commenters said the error bars were wide. Others are delighted with its speed on home hardware.

AI Daily Brief raises the safety question. It says looping moves reasoning inside the model's weights, making it cheaper to run but harder to observe, and that the major labs are embracing it anyway. There's one piece of evidence in the other direction. Latent Space cites a research variant in which destructive actions fell from about twenty-seven percent with one pass to about three percent with four.

OpenAI and Cisco released Shirudo, a free open-source tool that puts temporary shields on running Linux systems while the real patch is being rolled out. AI agents write the shields, operators approve them, and nothing needs restarting. OpenAI also resurfaced research from November fourth in which GPT six Cyber reproduced more than seventeen hundred Linux kernel vulnerabilities, with candidate fixes sent to maintainers.

A version of GPT six point two Sol tuned for conversation is rolling out in ChatGPT's Chat tab for paid plans. In OpenAI's own tests, image understanding rose from about forty-seven percent to nearly seventy-two, and instruction following on hard prompts went from sixty-two and a half to seventy-eight. World of AI found a catch in the system card: a score for following instructions to change default behavior fell from about sixty-one percent to forty-nine, so check your custom instructions before GPT five point six Sol leaves ChatGPT on December twenty-third.

OpenAI also launched OpenWorks, which pays local AI experts up to eight thousand dollars a month to help small businesses in twenty US cities. Owners get hands-on help at no cost, plus three months of ChatGPT Business. Software companies and firms backed by venture capital or private equity can't apply.

Prosecutors say former OpenAI engineer Abdulrahman Alfozan likely captured trade secrets with a fourteen-dollar device hidden behind a conference room TV. They've found hundreds of audio files, and they say he used OpenAI's own Codex to manage the recordings. He has pleaded not guilty, and his lawyers call him an "overly curious tinkerer."

The New York Times reports that Elon Musk has told SpaceX employees it's more lucrative to rent computing to rivals than to use it on Grok. Anthropic alone has agreed to spend up to a hundred twenty and a half billion dollars through twenty twenty-nine, and Musk renamed an engineering group chat "Sell Compute Because We Can't Compete." Separately, Virginia and Oregon sued over sexualized images generated by Grok, including images of children, and those are allegations at this stage.

A new benchmark called ShellBench tested agents on a hundred everyday tasks in messy workspaces, and even the best models failed about four in ten. GPT six Astra scored sixty-two and a half percent at about a dollar a run, while GPT six point one Sol scored sixty-one point four at seventeen cents. It comes from the OpenClaw Foundation, whose own harness was among those tested, and whose results put the open-source harnesses slightly ahead of Claude Code.

Anthropic's stock missed Monday's rally, and AI Daily Brief says it fell one point four percent to close just four dollars above its IPO price. It also says filings show each of the seven co-founders sold two billion dollars in shares, citing a post on X, while Forbes puts Dario Amodei's sale in the IPO at two hundred eighty million, so treat those figures carefully. Meanwhile, AI Secret reports that the European Central Bank estimates eight big AI spenders, Anthropic among them, have more than four trillion dollars in commitments off their balance sheets.

Google DeepMind used teams of Gemini four agents to find cheaper quantum algorithms, with the results formally verified in Lean. The newsletters differ on the fusion result, with Latent Space saying a ten point nine times reduction and TLDR AI saying eleven point eight. Photochemistry improved about eight times, and one drug-metabolism molecule two and a half.

OpenRouter dropped three providers, Together AI, Phala and Novita, after finding they leaked customer prompts and credentials. Together says an AI coding agent pushed a credential to public code, and someone used it to download stored prompts from June and July. OpenRouter also removed its zero data retention label from a hundred twenty-five endpoints.

And CrowdStrike is buying Zscaler for forty-three point nine billion dollars in stock, which The Rundown calls the largest cybersecurity deal on record. CrowdStrike expects five hundred million a year in cost cuts within two years of closing. The combined company would start with more than ten billion in annual recurring revenue.

Here's what connects today's stories for me: in almost every one, the sign-off arrived before the explanation. Nvidia's board cleared Huang while its own inquiry was still open, a proof was accepted before its author could say why it works, GLM five point four claimed its class without publishing a score, and OpenAI offered the industry a ruler that outsiders can't fully inspect. Set that beside a hundred and one complaints about checking AI's work, and the scarce job looks like the one that comes after the answer.
