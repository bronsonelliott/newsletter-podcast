Good morning, and welcome to Founders' Desk. Plenty happened overnight, and most of it comes down to people who build things deciding what comes next. That theme runs through almost every story below.

Lumen's chief executive, Dara Whitlock, is stepping down to spend more time on research. Paul Reyes, the company's chief operating officer, becomes interim CEO. Handoffs like this are always delicate, because the person leaving often holds the instincts the company was built on. Whitlock is choosing to go back toward the work that likely drew her in originally, which is a reasonable thing to want, though it also leaves a question hanging over the company's direction. Reyes is an operator by background, and that is a different kind of bet than keeping a visionary in the seat. I'll be watching closely over the next few weeks to see whether Lumen treats this as a caretaker period or a real change in what the company is trying to be.

Fennel said its coding agent has passed 1 million paying users. That number should make every developer-tools founder sit up. The important word is paying. Free signups are easy to collect, and plenty of agent products have looked enormous on launch day before quietly shrinking. People who keep paying after the novelty wears off are telling you the thing actually saves them time, and that's a much harder signal to fake. I'd argue this is the metric that matters most in the coding-agent category right now. Anyone can build a demo that impresses a crowd on social media. Keeping developers on a product through a full sprint, a bad release, and a budget review is the real test, and Fennel's figure suggests it's passing that test for a meaningful group of people.

Quill, which makes AI tools for court reporters, raised $85 million at a $600 million valuation. Court reporting is one of those unglamorous corners of the economy that nobody writes pitch decks about, and that's exactly why it's interesting. The buyer is clear, the costs are measurable, and the workflow can't tolerate sloppy output, since a transcript error can matter a great deal in a legal setting. Those constraints are uncomfortable for a startup, but they also make the product easier to judge. Investors backing a niche like this are signaling that they're looking past the obvious consumer categories and toward professional software with tight, well-understood needs. Whether Quill grows into its valuation is a question for the next few years. What I find encouraging is the willingness to take a serious bet on a market that most people in the Valley have never thought about.

Halcyon Labs announced a $2.1 billion funding round led by Brackwater Capital. The company makes AI for hospital scheduling. Scheduling sounds mundane until you've sat in a waiting room watching an exam room sit empty because the calendar was wrong. Hospitals run on thin margins and tight staffing, and small inefficiencies compound quickly across a large system. A round of this size is a strong statement about how much capital is willing to flow into healthcare operations, and about how seriously Brackwater takes that vertical. I read it as a sign that enterprise AI is moving out of the pilot stage and into permanent budget lines, which is where the real revenue has always been. It also raises the stakes for everyone else building in clinical software, because the bar for what counts as a credible healthcare AI company just went up.

That's the top of the stack. Four stories, one common thread: the companies that win this cycle are the ones with real customers, clear problems, and leaders who know what they're for. Lumen's leadership change is the one I'd keep an eye on, because it tests whether a research-driven founder can hand the daily work to an operator without losing the thread that made the company distinctive.

The hardware team in this profile had spent its first year selling the way it had always imagined it would. They built a unit they were proud of, priced it for a retail buyer who did not exist, and then waited for orders that came in at a trickle. By the end of that year, Teodora Vance, the company's head of operations, had stopped asking why customers weren't buying and started asking what they were actually trying to do.

The answer changed the company. Most of their early prospects were facilities managers at mid-sized warehouses who had no interest in owning a device. They wanted a problem solved for a season, measured against a number they already tracked, and then they wanted to decide whether it was worth keeping. A standard sale asked them to commit before they had any evidence. A pilot asked them to commit to a question.

So the team restructured around that question. Each pilot runs for sixty days on a fixed price, with a written definition of success agreed up front. If the customer hits the target, the pilot converts into a per-unit lease. If it misses, both sides walk away without a fight, and the team keeps the data. Vance says the first few pilots were close to a loss, because the setup work was expensive and the margins looked terrible on paper.

What turned it around was the conversion rate, which climbed fast once the criteria were clear. Buyers were no longer negotiating from fear of being stuck with something. Vance thinks that is the lesson most hardware founders miss: a pilot is not a discount on a sale. It is a product in its own right, and it needs its own design, pricing, and promise.
