Pocket Intelligence

Welcome back to Pocket Intelligence. It's the last full week before the holiday rush, and the device world is noisier than usual. Three bits of news landed in the past day, and I want to walk through them before we get to the main event: a long, slightly obsessive look at earbuds, which I have been testing for the past six weeks with more pairs than any sane person should own.

Let's start with Tessel, which will launch its phone assistant, Tessel Voice, worldwide on December 8. I have spent a fair amount of time with the early builds, and my honest take is that voice assistants on phones have been stuck in a strange middle zone for years. They can set a timer and read you the weather, but ask them anything with two or three steps in it and they fall apart. Tessel seems to be aiming at the part of the problem that actually annoys people, which is the back-and-forth. You can ask a follow-up without repeating yourself, and it keeps track of what you were talking about a minute ago. That sounds small. It is not small. Most of the friction in talking to a machine comes from having to restate context, and removing that is the difference between a gadget you use to be polite and one you use because it saves you time.

I still have reservations. Launching worldwide on one date is ambitious, and voice features live or die on how well they handle accents, dialects, and the mumbled grocery list you dictate while walking through a parking lot. I will reserve judgment until I can try it in a few cities and a few languages. What I can say is that the ambition is in the right place, and that the phone assistant category badly needed someone to try harder than "ask me anything, though I might not understand."

Next, Lumen has announced that its chief executive, Dara Whitlock, is stepping down to spend more time on research. Paul Reyes, the company's chief operating officer, becomes interim CEO. Executive changes are usually dull, but this one is worth a second look because Lumen has built its reputation on a particular kind of patience. Its products have never been the fastest to market or the flashiest on a spec sheet, and that was largely Whitlock's stamp. When a founder-style leader says she wants more time on research, it is hard not to read it as a signal about where the company's heart is. Whether that research pays off in products is the question that matters, and it is one we will not be able to answer from a press release.

Reyes is a known quantity in the operations side of the business. An interim title is exactly what it sounds like, so the real story will be what happens next, and I will be watching for it. For now, I would describe the moment as a pause in the company's public face rather than a change in its direction. That is my read, not a fact I can confirm, and I would be happy to be proven wrong.

Last, Verity has cut the price of its API by 40 percent, effective Monday. If you do not build software, you might skip past this one. Don't. API pricing is the hidden layer under a huge number of the apps and devices you use every day. When the cost of calling a service drops this sharply, developers stop rationing their experiments. Features that were too expensive to ship last quarter suddenly make sense, and smaller companies get a shot at things that were previously reserved for the well funded. Expect to see more of this in the coming months, in the form of gadgets that do a little more thinking without costing you a subscription. Verity has made a clear bet that cheaper access will grow its ecosystem faster than the lost margin hurts it, and it is a bet I find reasonable.

That's the news. Now, the thing I have been dreading writing, because I could happily spend ten thousand words on it.

Earbuds have become the most important consumer device most of us own that we barely think about. We wear them on commutes, on runs, on video calls, on the couch while someone else is trying to watch television. They are small enough to lose in a couch cushion and personal enough that we get attached to a particular pair the way we get attached to a favorite pen. Over the past six weeks I tested eleven pairs of earbuds across price points ranging from the cost of a decent lunch to the cost of a used car. I wore them on trains, in an office with a loud HVAC system, on a long walk through a rainy city, and in the gym where the bass goes to fight the clanging of weights. I also read the fine print on a lot of boxes, which I do not recommend as a hobby.

Let me start with the thing most people actually care about, which is sound. Earbud sound is a balance between three things that pull against each other: clarity, comfort, and how much the device tries to be clever. The cleverest pairs in this test used heavy processing to flatten out the sound and then add back what they thought you wanted to hear. Some of them were genuinely impressive for about ten minutes, and then the processing became noticeable. Vocals went thin, cymbals turned brittle, and the bass started to sound like it was being piped in from another room. The pairs I kept coming back to were the ones that got the basics right first. They reproduced a voice with weight and presence, they kept the midrange clear enough that a podcast did not sound like it was underwater, and they let the high end breathe without hissing.

Noise cancellation is where most reviews go to get lost in numbers, so I will try to describe it in human terms. Good active noise cancellation feels like someone quietly closing a door on the train's rumble. The low drone of an engine or an air conditioner fades, and the world does not get muffled in the process. The best of the eleven did this without a noticeable pressure change in the ears, which matters more than most people realize. Several cheaper models achieved a similar drop in volume, but they did it with a dull, pressurized feeling that made me want to take them out after forty minutes. The worst of the group had a hiss that you only noticed when a song paused, which is the kind of flaw that makes you distrust the whole product. Transparency mode, the feature that lets outside sound in so you can hear a barista call your name, varied even more. A few sounded natural. Others made every voice sound like it was coming through a cardboard tube.

A note for readers in Europe. Tessel's support page now says Tessel Voice will not reach the European Union until March. The December 8 date is for everywhere else.

Fit is the category that decides whether you will ever use a pair again. Earbuds are a deeply personal fit problem, and no review can tell you how a given tip will sit in your particular ear canal. What I can tell you is what I observed. Four of the eleven had a stem or body shape that caught on my outer ear after an hour, which made long calls uncomfortable. Two had tips that sealed well at first and then slowly loosened, forcing me to re-seat them every twenty minutes. The most comfortable pair in the set was also one of the least expensive, a fact I found mildly annoying as a reviewer who likes to be surprised by expensive things that are worth the money. It had a lightweight body and a soft silicone tip that disappeared in my ear after a while, which is exactly what you want.

Battery life deserves its own paragraph because the numbers on the box are almost always optimistic. In my testing, the listed playing time was reached only under ideal conditions: moderate volume, noise cancellation off, and a single long track. Real-world use with noise cancellation on and a phone call or two shaved a meaningful chunk off that figure. The case also matters more than people think. A case that charges quickly and holds enough extra charge to cover a weekend trip is worth more than an extra hour of single-charge playtime. Two models in the test had lovely earbuds stuck inside cases that took nearly two hours to refill, and that kind of friction adds up every single day.

Call quality is the part of the review that many people skip and then regret. Most earbuds have microphones good enough for a quick check-in, but few can hold up on a noisy street or in a windy park. I made calls from a busy sidewalk, a bus shelter during a gust, and a kitchen with a dishwasher running. The strongest performers used beamforming microphones and a bit of processing to suppress the wind and the clatter, and they did not sound like they were being filtered into oblivion. The weakest made me sound like I was calling from inside a metal bucket. If you take a lot of calls on the go, this is the single most important specification to read about and test yourself.

Software is the other half of the experience, and it is where the newest pairs are trying to win. Companion apps now handle equalizer presets, firmware updates, and custom controls, and some of them add features that lean on the phone's own intelligence. A few of the pairs I tested did a good job of letting you customize squeezes and taps without digging through five menus. Others buried basic settings behind an account signup and an update that never seemed to finish. I have a simple rule for software in earbuds: if I have to open an app to answer a call or pause a song, the app had better be fast and stable. Several failed that test, and it cost them points in my final ranking.

So which ones made the cut? I will tell you the broad picture now and save the detailed pick-by-pick breakdown for the next section. The best overall pair was not the most expensive, the most feature-packed, or the one with the most marketing behind it. It was the one that sounded clear, fit comfortably for hours, cancelled noise without hiss or pressure, and charged quickly from a case that felt sturdy in the hand. The runner-up was more ambitious but stumbled on fit. A bargain pick surprised me by outperforming its price tier on almost every measure except software polish, which I think is a fair trade. And the one I would recommend to anyone who mostly wants to forget their earbuds exist was a quiet, unfussy model that never asked for attention, which is perhaps the highest compliment I can give a device of this kind.

Grab a pair you already own, put it in, and keep reading.
